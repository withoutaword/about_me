
# **The Long Way Home**  
_In Memory of My Grandmother_

## I. My Grandmother

My last memory of my grandmother is from a scorching day in 2016, shortly after the Mid-Autumn Festival. It was the day of her funeral.

The sunlight was dazzling, almost harsh, falling across the open ground in front of the ancestral hall. The funeral was somehow both busy and quiet. A heavy, greasy smell hung in the hot air. People moved around attending to different tasks, speaking little, while beside the coffin a funeral band played the piercing notes of the suona over the steady beating of drums.

In a corner of the ancestral hall stood a refrigerated coffin.

My grandmother lay quietly inside.

That corner was where her long life had come to an end. On the night of the Mid-Autumn Festival, she had taken her final breath there.

Two years earlier, I had been standing on the very same ground, but the scene was entirely different.

It was a cold night, dark as ink. Our clan had invited a local opera troupe to perform Baizi Opera, a traditional form of opera from our hometown. Rows of elderly villagers sat on wooden benches in front of the stage, listening intently.

And there, right in the front row, sat my grandmother.

Wrapped tightly in a thick padded coat, she had arrived early to secure her place and stayed until the troupe finished late at night. When the performance ended, I took her hand and slowly guided her through the narrow lanes beneath the dim yellow streetlights, step by careful step, back to my uncle's house.

I remember how deep the night was.

How cold the air felt.

And how light my grandmother seemed in my hand.

If I turn the clock further back, the memories become warmer.

My grandfather died early, so in my childhood memories, there was mostly just my grandmother.

When I was seven or eight, my two brothers and I often played with toys at home or shot marbles in the narrow alley beside our house. Every so often, we would see my grandmother walking past, carrying a large red plastic bag.

She would hurry into the house, reach into the bag, and pull out bananas, fruit and small cakes, pressing them into our hands.

They were offerings she had bought for the gods. After finishing her prayers, she would bring the food to us on her way past.

She was never particularly careful about small things. When buying fruit from the little shops nearby, she rarely inspected it closely, so the bananas and other fruit she brought us were sometimes already bruised or beginning to spoil.

We didn't care.

To children, fruit and cakes were still treasures, and we always looked forward to seeing her arrive with that red bag.

She would put everything down, remind us of one thing or another, and soon be on her way again—most likely heading to another temple to pray.

If my mother happened to be home, my grandmother would sometimes ask her for a little money to buy incense or make an offering.

She often asked her children for money, but rarely to buy anything for herself. Most of it went toward fruit, cakes and other offerings, or incense, candles and paper ingots for worship.

Sometimes, when she ran out of money, she even bought these things on credit.

One day, a shopkeeper came to my uncle asking to be paid. Only then did the family discover that my grandmother had quietly accumulated quite a considerable debt.

After that, the adults began restricting how much money she could spend. They even warned the shops she frequented not to let her buy anything on credit anymore.

## II. Hong Kong

I was told that my grandmother had once been to Hong Kong when she was young. I no longer remember exactly why she went there.

But she was proud of that chapter of her life.

Whenever she spoke about Hong Kong, her face seemed to light up.

Unfortunately, we were never particularly interested. We never really sat down with her and asked her to tell us about those colourful years of her youth.

Looking back now, I wish we had.

In her time, travelling as far as Hong Kong was extraordinary. Many people in her generation spent their entire lives without ever leaving the county where they were born. Yet somehow, she had made her way to one of the most prosperous and cosmopolitan cities in Asia.

No wonder she looked so proud whenever she talked about it.

Once, she spoke Cantonese with one of my university friends. Her pronunciation was surprisingly clear and authentic—better, in fact, than that of many of us younger people.

I remember being genuinely surprised.

In her later years, I would often see her when I went walking near the open ground beside our home. There was a small temple nearby, and she was frequently there.

Whenever we met, she would reach into her pocket and take out a sweet for me.

Then she would take another one and put it into her own mouth.

Most elderly people I knew rarely ate sweets, but my grandmother, already in her nineties, still liked them.

Sometimes I wonder whether she had discovered something very simple about life: when life becomes bitter, perhaps having something sweet in your mouth makes it feel just a little less so.

I don't know whether that was what she thought.

But I like to imagine that it was.

Not long afterwards, my grandmother developed dementia.

My grandmother on my father's side once told me that one day she walked out of the house as usual, but before long she began looking confused. Someone from the clan saw her wandering and asked where she was going.

"I'm going home," she said.

But she had forgotten where home was.

From that day on, the family stopped letting her go out alone.

She began spending most of her days sitting at home watching television. Often they were romantic dramas filled with young actors and actresses. She couldn't understand Mandarin, but somehow she watched them with the same fascination with which she had once watched Baizi Opera.

At the time, I came home quite often, and whenever I did, I would spend some time with her.

Her memory had already begun to drift in and out. Sometimes she couldn't even remember my name.

Yet occasionally, when her mind became clear again, she would wait until no one else was around and quietly ask me the same question she had asked so many times before:

"Did you bring any money?"

She still wanted a little money for incense and temple offerings.

I would feel slightly helpless, but I always gave her some.

She would take the money and carefully tuck it into an inner pocket of her clothes, as if protecting something precious.

Eventually, every road reaches its end.

Not long after the dementia began, my grandmother fell at home and fractured her femur. I went with my uncle to find a doctor, but there was little that could be done. The doctor prescribed some painkillers.

So she remained in bed, enduring the pain.

Age and illness slowly consumed her body. She grew thinner and weaker.

Watching her suffer was painful. We wanted to help, but there was very little any of us could do.

Finally, on the night of the Mid-Autumn Festival in 2016, inside the ancestral hall, my grandmother breathed out for the last time.

Her ninety-three-year journey through this world had come to an end.

## III. The Last Goodbye

On the day of the funeral, I returned home and changed into plain mourning clothes.

Strangely, I felt calm.

She had lived a long life, richer in experience than many of her generation, and I had watched illness and old age gradually take away her strength. Perhaps, I thought, saying goodbye was not necessarily the worst thing.

I comforted my mother, whose eyes were swollen from crying, and my cousin, who was quietly weeping in a corner.

Under the hot autumn sun, I tried to do whatever I could for my grandmother's final journey.

I helped carry supplies needed for the funeral. When I returned, I insisted on kneeling beside her coffin and taking part in a farewell ritual that was traditionally reserved for members of her own clan.

After noon, I helped organise the funeral procession.

At the crematorium, I waited for her ashes and helped gather her belongings. I received her death certificate with my own hands.

That evening, I joined the family for the final farewell ceremony.

As I knelt before her portrait, something on the offering table suddenly caught my eye.

Fruit.

And small cakes.

For a moment, everything around me seemed to disappear.

I saw her again.

An old woman walking quickly toward our house, a red plastic bag swinging from her hand.

She reached inside, took out the fruit and cakes, and pressed them into our hands. She reminded us of something, as she always did, then turned around and walked away.

Except this time,

she would never come back.

My tears suddenly broke through.

I pressed my hands against my face, trying desperately to stop them.

But I couldn't.

## IV. Home

Grandma,

you walked a long road through this world.

I hope that now,

you have finally found your way back

to the home you never stopped searching for.
