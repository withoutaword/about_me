title: Leaving a Mark on History
date: 2026-07-11

When I had just graduated from university, I joined a well-known company and met a mentor who had started his career there a few years before me. By then, he had already been promoted to a mid-level position.

During a casual conversation one day, he told me about a dream he once had:

**to leave a mark on history.**

I was surprised. I had never heard anyone around me describe such a thing as a life goal—to leave something behind in history, as Li Bai, Su Shi, or Lu Xun had done.

At first, I could hardly suppress a smile.

My colleague seemed slightly embarrassed after saying it aloud, perhaps realizing how unrealistic it sounded.

Yet the more I thought about it in the years that followed, the less absurd it seemed. In fact, I gradually came to see it as an aspiration worthy of profound respect.

No matter how much wealth, status, or luxury we acquire, we can enjoy them only for the few decades we are alive. Even a vast fortune passed down to our children will eventually disperse after several generations.

But if we manage to leave even the faintest trace in history, something changes.

A part of our existence crosses the boundary of time.

Someone who has never met us may one day encounter our words, our ideas, or something we created—and know that we were once here.

That was when I began to understand the true scale of my mentor's dream.

It was, in its own way, a magnificent one.

---

Yet we now live in an age of algorithms, relentless iteration, and instant returns. Everything accelerates. We move so quickly that we often find ourselves trapped in a kind of collective forgetfulness—and, perhaps, a collective sense of meaninglessness.

Recently, that comfortable rhythm was abruptly interrupted by the news that Dr. Gao had passed away at only fifty-five.

He had already achieved financial independence. But wealth alone is rarely enough to resist time. Had he simply lived an ordinary life and disappeared quietly from the world, then decades or a century from now, perhaps only the occasional remembrance of his family and the fading memories of friends would remain.

Eventually, time would erase almost everything.

But as a macroeconomist, Dr. Gao was fortunate in another, much deeper sense.

He did not remain hidden behind the dry numbers of an ivory tower. Instead, he stepped into the turbulence of his time and compressed an extraordinarily complicated cross-section of society into just three sentences:

> “The elderly, full of vitality; the young, lifeless and weary; and the middle-aged, with little desire left for life.”

These were not cold doctrines buried in an economics textbook.

They were words that ordinary people could understand—the people exhausted in office cubicles, the people staring silently into the darkness late at night, wondering how much longer they could keep going.

From this perspective, history has already remembered him.

What may preserve his name is not necessarily the scale of his academic achievements or the size of his fortune, but the fact that he captured something essential about an era and preserved it in a remarkably concentrated form.

I believe that many years from now, when people try to understand the period through which we are living today, these three sentences will continue to resurface.

The history of these years reminds me of the Trisolarans in *The Three-Body Problem* when they enter their dehydrated state.

An era, once compressed by time, can become thin and flat, almost like a sheet of paper. But pour these three sentences over it, and suddenly the world begins to rehydrate.

It expands.

It regains its shape.

It becomes vivid and alive again.

There will, of course, be enormous historical archives in the future. There will be rigorous academic studies, detailed statistics, and grand narratives explaining our age.

Yet hidden behind these few simple sentences are countless fragments of real life:

the employee badge of a thirty-five-year-old tech worker facing yet another round of corporate “optimization”;

the quiet anxiety of a mortgage hanging over someone walking out of an office tower;

the red taillights of ride-hailing drivers competing for one more order deep into the night;

even the Hermès belt that suddenly snaps in the middle of what had seemed like an unstoppable rise.

Someday, someone may encounter those three sentences and, through them, pull the living soul of our era out from beneath the dust of history.

In that sense, Dr. Gao succeeded.

He left his own unmistakable mark in the long river of history.

---

Thinking about this brought me back to that mentor from many years ago.

He and his wife eventually had two children. They bought an apartment in Shenzhen. He became, in many ways, a typical member of the middle class—moving steadily along the tracks of the system, carefully balancing work, family, expenses, and the countless practical calculations of everyday life.

I, too, am moving forward, trying to find my place in the enormous wave of the AI era.

And suddenly I found myself wanting to travel back to that conversation and tell him:

**That dream you had back then may not be as unreachable as we thought.**

It is certainly not easy.

To leave a mark on history requires more than intelligence, ability, or recognition.

It also requires the courage to speak when silence is easier.

Perhaps that courage does not require us to become tragic heroes.

Perhaps it is something much smaller.

It is the willingness to honestly record the pain of an individual while standing beneath the enormous gears of a system.

It is the ability to remain sensitive to the lives of real people around us, even amid the noise and trivialities of everyday life.

Sometimes, all that remains of an era is a single sentence that dared to puncture the silence.

And if that sentence is honest enough,

if it reaches deeply enough into the reality of its time,

then it may acquire something extraordinarily rare:

**the power to cross time—and resist being forgotten.**
